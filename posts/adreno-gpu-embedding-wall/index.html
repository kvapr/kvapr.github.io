Why LLMs won't load on the Adreno GPU: the 256 MiB embedding wall

Two months of attempts, three Qualcomm SoCs, one tensor, and one driver number that no document mentions.

Before this series settled on the NPU, I spent about two months trying to serve language models on the Adreno GPU, first on a QCS6490 and then on the QCS9075, through llama.cpp's OpenCL backend. Every attempt died at model load. This post is the root cause and everything found around it: the single tensor that fails, why quantization does not help, where the 256 MiB limit comes from, what I measured on two boards, what the ecosystem has done about it since, and the one configuration nobody has tried. It is the GPU deep dive behind the memory-hierarchy post. Boards: Dragonwing IQ-9075 EVK with the Adreno 663, on Qualcomm Linux 1.5 and 1.6; earlier attempts on the QCS6490's Adreno 643. Confidence labels as usual: measured, documented, inferred.

Why try the GPU at all

The appeal was never speed. If the language model ran on the GPU, the NPU would be free for other work, and in an edge product that other work is video analytics. One box, a camera pipeline on the accelerator, an assistant on the graphics core. The Adreno 663 is a capable OpenCL device, llama.cpp has an OpenCL backend that Qualcomm itself maintains, and the models are small. It looked like the easy half of the design.

The symptom

Every model in the lineup died at load with an allocation failure. Not slow, not wrong: unloaded. The behavior was identical on the QCS6490 and the QCS9075, and the same cap later showed up on the QCS8550, so it was not one board or one driver build. Root-causing it took three independent checks that agreed: a custom parser over the GGUF files, the reference gguf-py tooling, and llama.cpp's own runtime output. All three pointed at the same place.

The cause: one tensor

Adreno's OpenCL driver caps a single allocation at 256 MiB. llama.cpp does not need a model to fit in one allocation; it packs a model across as many buffers as it takes, and whole models far larger than 256 MiB load fine. What cannot be split is a single tensor, and in every model tested exactly one tensor exceeds the cap: the token-embedding matrix. No attention or MLP tensor comes close, even at 16-bit precision for 3B and 4B models.

Three things follow from that. The embedding's size is driven by vocabulary size, not parameter count, so a larger vocabulary makes a smaller model harder to load. Quantization does not rescue it, because llama.cpp keeps embeddings at a higher precision than the rest of a 4-bit model; the sizes in the figure are from Q4 files. And a newer model is more likely to hit the wall than an older one, since vocabularies have grown: Llama-3.2-3B's 128K tokens give a 308 MB embedding, Phi-4-Mini's 200K give 481 MB, Qwen3.5-4B's 248K give 497 MB.

The consequence, measured: no model in the lineup loads with full GPU offload without either splitting the embedding or leaving it on the CPU.

Where 256 comes from

The number is not a hardware limit, and it is not in Qualcomm's OpenCL guide. Adreno 6xx drivers report 1 GiB of global memory regardless of the physical RAM behind them, and the OpenCL specification's minimum for the largest single allocation is a quarter of global memory. A quarter of 1 GiB is 256 MiB. That mechanism is inferred from the specification and the reported values; the values themselves are measured. On 27 August I probed two IQ-9075 boards, one on Qualcomm Linux 1.5 and one on 1.6:

Two releases apart, the GPU stack did not move. These are, as far as I can find, the first public capability dumps for the Adreno 663. Newer Adreno generations, the 7xx and 8xx parts in current phones, report a 1 GiB cap instead, still a fixed number rather than one that scales with RAM.

What the ecosystem did about it

More than I expected, and none of it reaches this silicon yet.

llama.cpp gained a dedicated escape hatch in March 2026, days after my testing window: an opt-in path that asks the driver for a large buffer through a Qualcomm extension. It needs two things, OpenCL 3.0 and the driver-side extension. The boards have the first, which was a surprise; the research had assumed 2.x from the platform documentation, and the probe corrected it. They lack the second, and the extension is stated for the 7xx and 8xx generations. So the ask to Qualcomm for these parts shrinks to one item: ship the extension.

Vulkan looked like the other way around the cap, and it is not. llama.cpp's Vulkan floor dropped to 1.2, so the 1.2.140 driver passes the version check on paper. In practice every report I could find of llama.cpp's Vulkan backend on any Adreno fails: load failures, lost devices, or gibberish, and the proprietary driver has its own wall, a 128 MiB limit on how much of a buffer one descriptor can see. The live Vulkan lane is the open-source Mesa driver, Turnip, which reaches Vulkan 1.3 and ships in Ubuntu with GPU firmware for the QCS6490 and SA8775P families. Whether it runs on the QCS9075 is unevidenced, and whether llama.cpp's Vulkan kernels then behave on Adreno is a separate question nobody has answered.

Would it even be worth it

Suppose the wall were gone. The GPU shares the board's DRAM and its bandwidth with the CPU and the NPU, and decode is bandwidth-bound, so the GPU cannot beat the other two where it matters most for chat. The best published Adreno decode figure for an 8B model, on a newer flagship part, is about 13 tokens per second, in the same range as this board's NPU. On speed alone the GPU offered nothing.

Two counterweights keep the question alive. One 2026 survey measured an Adreno as the most energy-efficient decoder per token in its sample, ahead of a discrete laptop GPU and Apple's Metal path; for a device on a power budget that is the number that matters. And the GPU is the one accelerator present on every Qualcomm tier, which the NPU is not. The reason to want the GPU lane was never a faster LLM. It was a second lane, so the NPU can hold the camera pipeline while the language model runs elsewhere.

A model under the cap, untested

The cap only kills single tensors over 256 MiB, and I can find no published attempt at the obvious escape. A 4B-class model with a modest vocabulary may have an embedding under the cap, in which case it loads on the GPU today, on stock Qualcomm Linux, with no driver change. The check is a minute's work: inspect the GGUF, find the largest tensor, compare it with 256 MiB, then attempt the load. I have not run it yet. Nor can I find any published attempt of a language model on the Adreno GPU beside a vision model on the Hexagon NPU on any Snapdragon or Dragonwing part. That configuration is the experiment this series is building toward, and this post is its baseline.

Transferable principles

  • On Adreno, the OpenCL limit that matters is per allocation, not per model. Check the largest single tensor before assuming a model will not fit.
  • Vocabulary size, not parameter count, decides whether a model clears the wall. Newer models with larger vocabularies are more exposed, and quantization does not help, because embeddings stay at higher precision.
  • The 256 MiB figure is a driver policy derived from a hardcoded 1 GiB global-memory report, not a property of the RAM. It is undocumented; measure it on your board.
  • The upstream fix exists and does not apply here. Before relying on an escape hatch, check the driver extension list, not the OpenCL version.
  • A GPU lane on this class of board is about a second tenant for the chip, not a faster model. Judge it by what it frees, and by energy per token, not by decode speed.
